Kitchen Backsplash Ideas & Tile Cost in Hickory, NC

Backsplash & Tile Updated September 2026 6 min read

The backsplash is the smallest line item in most kitchen remodels and one of the most visible. It's the upgrade with the best ratio of impact to cost — provided you pick a tile that suits how you cook and a grout that won't look dirty in two years.

A kitchen backsplash covers maybe 30 to 40 square feet — a fraction of the surface area of your cabinets or floor. Yet it sits at eye level, directly behind the range and sink where you spend most of your time, which is why it does so much to define how a kitchen reads. It's also the one element homeowners in Hickory most often update on its own, without touching anything else in the room.

Backsplash Tile Types Worth Considering

Material affects durability, cleaning, and cost roughly in that order:

  • Ceramic subway tile. The default for good reason — inexpensive, easy to clean, and it hasn't looked dated in a century. A 3×6 in a running bond is the classic; larger formats and a vertical stack read more current.
  • Porcelain. Denser and less porous than ceramic, so it handles grease and moisture better. Large-format porcelain means fewer grout lines, which is the single best thing you can do for long-term maintenance.
  • Natural stone (marble, travertine, slate). Beautiful and genuinely one-of-a-kind, but porous. Behind a range it needs sealing and will stain if you don't keep up with it. Best on a wall that isn't directly behind the cooktop.
  • Glass tile. Non-porous, reflects light, and makes a small or dark kitchen feel larger. It shows every imperfection in the wall beneath it, so substrate prep matters more than with any other material.
  • Mosaic sheets. Small tiles pre-mounted on mesh backing. Fast to install over large areas and great for a feature section, though all those grout lines need a quality grout to stay clean.
  • Slab backsplash. The same stone as your countertop, run up the wall. Zero grout lines, seamless, and the easiest surface in the kitchen to wipe down. Costs the most, and pairs naturally with new countertop installation since it's cut from the same material.

How High Should the Backsplash Go?

Standard is 18 inches — the gap between the countertop and the bottom of the upper cabinets. Two variations are worth considering:

  • Full height behind the range. Running tile from counter to hood or ceiling in the cooking zone creates a focal point and puts a wipeable surface exactly where grease actually lands.
  • Floor-to-ceiling feature wall. On a wall without upper cabinets — often behind a sink under a window, or on an open-shelving wall — tiling the full height makes the kitchen feel deliberate rather than partially finished.
  • 4-inch counter backsplash. A short strip of countertop material along the wall. Inexpensive and protects against splashes, but it reads as builder-grade in an otherwise updated kitchen.

Grout: The Choice That Decides How It Ages

Homeowners spend weeks choosing tile and thirty seconds choosing grout, which is backwards. Grout is what gets dirty, and it's what makes a five-year-old backsplash look tired:

  • Epoxy grout is non-porous, stain-resistant, and needs no sealing. It costs more and takes more skill to install, and in a cooking zone it's almost always worth both.
  • Sealed cement grout is the standard and performs well when properly sealed — and re-sealed periodically.
  • Grout color matters as much as grout type. Matching the tile makes the wall read as one surface; contrasting emphasizes the pattern. Bright white grout behind a range will show every splatter, so mid-tones are the practical choice in the cooking zone.
  • Thinner grout lines mean less to clean. Rectified tile allows tighter joints than standard tile.
Nobody notices a good backsplash installation. They notice the crooked row, the grout line that wanders, and the outlet cutout that doesn't sit flush behind the cover plate.

What a Good Installation Actually Involves

Tile is unforgiving — every error is permanent once the mortar sets. A proper backsplash installation starts with checking the wall for flat and plumb and correcting it if needed, because no amount of care at the tile stage fixes a wavy wall. From there it's a laid-out dry run to determine where cuts land, full-coverage mortar so there are no hollow spots, outlet boxes extended so cover plates sit flush against the tile rather than recessed, and clean cuts around windows and end runs finished with a proper edge profile or bullnose instead of exposed tile edges.

Pairing the Backsplash With the Rest of the Kitchen

The backsplash is the piece that ties cabinets and countertops together, so it's the last thing to choose, not the first. A busy granite pairs best with a quiet tile; a solid-color quartz can carry a more expressive backsplash. If you're replacing counters at the same time, choose the slab first and the tile second — there are thousands of tiles and far fewer slabs. Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a kitchen backsplash cost in Hickory, NC?

Most backsplash installations run $1,000 to $2,500 for a typical 30 to 40 square foot area, or roughly $25 to $60 per square foot installed depending on tile type and pattern complexity.

How high should a kitchen backsplash go?

Standard height is 18 inches, filling the space between countertop and upper cabinets. Running tile to the ceiling behind the range or on a feature wall is a popular upgrade that adds material and labor but creates a strong focal point.

What is the easiest backsplash tile to keep clean?

Large-format porcelain or ceramic with minimal grout lines, since grout is what collects grease and discoloration. Pairing any tile with epoxy grout or a quality sealed grout makes a real difference in a cooking zone.

How long does backsplash installation take?

Most backsplashes are tiled in a single day, with grouting the following day once the mortar has set. Intricate patterns like herringbone or mosaic sheets can stretch the work to three days.
