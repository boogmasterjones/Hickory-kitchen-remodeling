Cabinet Refacing vs. Replacing in Hickory, NC: Which Is Right for Your Kitchen?

Cabinets Updated September 2026 7 min read

Cabinets are the biggest line item in almost every kitchen remodel — which makes refacing versus replacing the single most consequential decision in your budget. The good news: there's a straightforward test that tells you which one your kitchen actually needs.

Call (941) 327-9667 See Our Cabinet Services

Homeowners searching for cabinet refacing in Hickory, NC are usually weighing it against full replacement and trying to figure out whether the cheaper option is a compromise. It isn't — but only when the conditions are right. Refacing a kitchen with failing cabinet boxes is money thrown away, and replacing perfectly sound boxes is money left on the table. Here's how to tell the difference.

What Refacing and Replacing Actually Mean

The terms get used loosely, so it's worth being precise:

  • Cabinet refacing keeps your existing cabinet boxes in place and replaces every visible surface. You get brand-new doors and drawer fronts, new hinges and hardware, and matching wood veneer or laminate applied over the exposed face frames and end panels. When it's finished, everything you can see is new.
  • Cabinet replacement removes the old cabinetry entirely and installs new boxes, doors, and drawers. It's the only path if you want to change the layout, resize openings, add a pantry, or move to a different cabinet configuration.
  • Cabinet painting is a third, cheaper option — the boxes and doors stay, and everything is prepped and refinished. It refreshes color but not door style, and the finish is only as durable as the prep work beneath it.

The Five-Minute Test: Are Your Boxes Worth Keeping?

Go to your kitchen and check these five things. They tell you almost everything:

  • Press on the box sides and floor. Solid plywood or well-sealed particleboard should feel firm with no give. Soft, swollen, or crumbling material — especially under the sink — means the boxes are past saving.
  • Look for water damage. Check the cabinet floor beneath the sink and dishwasher. Dark staining, bubbling, or delaminating layers are disqualifying.
  • Check whether the shelves sag. A slight bow under heavy dishes is normal and fixable. A permanent curve means the material has failed.
  • Test the doors and frames for square. Open a door and see whether it hangs evenly. Racked or out-of-square frames won't hold new doors properly.
  • Ask whether the layout works. This one has nothing to do with condition. If you need to move the sink, widen a run, or add a pantry, refacing can't do it — no matter how good the boxes are.

Pass all five and refacing is almost certainly the better value. Fail the first four, and new cabinets are the sounder investment. Fail only the fifth, and you're really asking a layout question, not a cabinet question.

When Refacing Is the Right Call

Refacing tends to be the better decision when:

  • Your cabinet boxes are solid and the layout already works for how you cook.
  • You're happy with the current configuration but hate the dated door style or finish.
  • You want the kitchen usable through most of the project — the boxes never leave, so there's no week of empty walls.
  • You'd rather redirect the savings into countertops, a tile backsplash, or better appliances.
  • You're updating to sell and want maximum visual impact per dollar.

When Replacement Is Worth the Extra Cost

Replacement earns its price when:

  • The boxes show water damage, sagging, or structural failure — refacing a failing box just puts a new door on a problem.
  • You're changing the layout, moving appliances, or adding an island.
  • You want deeper drawers, full-extension slides, soft-close throughout, or modern organization that existing boxes can't accommodate.
  • Your current cabinets are builder-grade particleboard nearing the end of their service life.
  • You're gutting the kitchen anyway — at that point, the incremental cost of new boxes is much smaller.
Refacing isn't a shortcut — it's the right answer for a sound kitchen with a dated face. The mistake is refacing boxes that should have been replaced, then paying twice five years later.

What Refacing Actually Involves

A proper reface is more involved than most homeowners expect. The crew measures every door and drawer opening, removes the old doors and hardware, preps and cleans the face frames, applies matching veneer or laminate to every exposed surface including end panels and the sides of the cabinet runs, then hangs new doors and drawer fronts with new hinges and adjusts every one for even gaps and alignment. Frequently Asked Questions

How much does cabinet refacing cost in Hickory, NC?

Refacing typically runs $4,000 to $10,000 for an average Hickory-area kitchen, versus $10,000 to $25,000 or more for full replacement. Door and drawer count, door style, and whether you choose wood veneer or laminate determine where you land.

How do I know if my cabinets can be refaced?

Press firmly on the box sides, shelves, and floor. Solid boxes, square frames, no sagging shelves, and no water damage or delamination mean you're a good candidate. Soft or swollen particleboard, sagging shelves, or boxes pulling away from the wall mean replacement is the better investment.

How long does cabinet refacing take?

Most refacing projects finish in three to five days, compared to two to three weeks for full replacement. Because the boxes stay put, your kitchen stays largely usable throughout.

Does cabinet refacing look as good as new cabinets?

Yes, when it's done properly. Every surface you see afterward is new — doors, drawer fronts, hardware, and matching veneer on the exposed frames and end panels. The only original components are the boxes hidden behind the doors.
